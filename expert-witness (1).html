Qatar's cybersecurity laws at a glance

The laws that most often shape cybercrime cases and digital-evidence disputes in Qatar. Select one to see what it covers and where an expert helps.

Law No. 14 of 2014Cybercrime Prevention Law

Criminalises offences committed through information technology, including unauthorised access to systems, interception, damage to data or systems, electronic fraud and forgery, and offences committed online such as privacy violations.

Where an expert helps: showing how access happened and from which device or account, and whether the digital evidence is authentic and complete.

Law No. 13 of 2016Personal Data Privacy Protection Law

Sets obligations on organisations that process personal data, including appropriate security measures, breach notification and individuals' rights over their data.

Where an expert helps: establishing the scope and cause of a breach, and whether reasonable safeguards were in place.

Decree-Law No. 16 of 2010Electronic Transactions and Commerce Law

Gives legal recognition to electronic records, messages, contracts and signatures, subject to conditions on their reliability.

Where an expert helps: proving whether WhatsApp messages, emails and electronic contracts are genuine, and in online buying and selling disputes.

National frameworkNCSA strategy and NIA standard

The National Cyber Security Agency oversees national cybersecurity, including the National Cyber Security Strategy 2024–2030 and the National Information Assurance (NIA) standard used by organisations in Qatar.

Where an expert helps: assessing whether an organisation's controls met recognised national expectations at the time of an incident.

Frequently asked questions

What does a cybersecurity expert witness do?

A cybersecurity expert witness examines digital evidence and explains, independently and in plain language, what it shows. That can mean reconstructing how an attacker entered a network, identifying the malware involved, establishing a timeline from logs, or assessing whether evidence was collected and preserved properly. The expert's duty is to the court, not to the party that appointed them.

When should a lawyer or company involve a forensics expert?

As early as possible, ideally before systems are rebuilt or devices are wiped. Early involvement protects volatile evidence, keeps a defensible chain of custody, and avoids well-meaning actions that destroy the data a case will later depend on.

Can you prove whether a WhatsApp chat is genuine?

Often, yes. Screenshots alone are easy to fabricate, but a forensic examination of the phone can recover the underlying chat database, timestamps, deleted or edited messages and the device and number involved. This allows an expert to state whether the conversation shown in court matches what is actually stored on the device.

Can you check whether an email is authentic or forged?

Yes. By analysing the full message headers, authentication results (SPF, DKIM and DMARC), the servers the email passed through and the mailbox logs, an expert can show whether an email was really sent by the claimed sender or was spoofed, altered or sent from a look-alike domain.

Do you assess the value and condition of electronic devices in disputes?

Yes. In buying and selling disputes, courts often need to know whether a phone, laptop, server or software matched the agreed specification, whether a defect existed before the sale, and what the device was worth at a given date. The assessment is based on inspection, specifications, purchase records and current market prices.

Which Qatari laws apply to cybercrime and digital evidence?

The main ones are Law No. 14 of 2014 on combating cybercrime, Law No. 13 of 2016 on protecting personal data privacy, and Decree-Law No. 16 of 2010 on electronic transactions and commerce, which recognises electronic records and signatures. The National Cyber Security Agency oversees the national framework, including the National Cyber Security Strategy 2024–2030 and the National Information Assurance standard.
