How AI can catch Android ransomware before it locks your phone

Ransomware no longer targets only office networks. On Android phones it can lock the screen, encrypt photos and documents, and demand payment within minutes of installation. Here is how machine learning can spot it before it does damage.

By Dr. Moutaz Alazab · 24 September 2026 · 5 min read

Why phones are an attractive target

Phones hold our banking apps, work email, family photos and authentication codes. Users install apps from many sources, and a malicious app can look almost identical to a legitimate one. Traditional antivirus depends on signatures of known samples, so it struggles with new or slightly modified ransomware, which is exactly what attackers release.

What an app gives away before it runs

Every Android app declares what it wants to do. It requests permissions, such as access to storage or the ability to draw over other apps, and it calls system functions (APIs) for things like encryption, device administration and network access. Ransomware tends to request an unusual combination of these. My earlier work with colleagues showed that permission requests and API calls alone carry enough signal to separate malicious apps from benign ones with a machine-learning classifier.

The difficulty is not a lack of features. It is having thousands of them, most of which are noise.

Choosing the features that matter

A typical app can be described by thousands of possible features. Feeding them all to a model makes it slow, harder to explain and more likely to learn coincidences. This is where nature-inspired optimisation helps. Algorithms such as the Jaya algorithm, Grey Wolf Optimizer and Harris Hawks Optimisation search through combinations of features and keep the small set that best separates ransomware from normal apps.

In my research I have used a binary Jaya algorithm and a hybrid of simultaneous swarm-based optimisers for exactly this task, published in Expert Systems and Cognitive Computation in 2024. The result is a compact detector that is fast enough to run on the device itself and simple enough to explain which behaviours triggered the alarm.

Why lightweight and explainable matters

A detector that needs a data centre is of little use on a phone. A detector that cannot explain itself is of little use in court or to a security team deciding what to block. Small feature sets make both possible: the model runs locally, and an analyst can see that, for example, an app combined device-admin rights, bulk file access and cryptographic calls with no plausible reason.

What you can do today

  • Install apps only from official stores, and check the developer and reviews.
  • Be suspicious of any app that asks for device administrator rights or to draw over other apps without a clear reason.
  • Keep automatic backups of photos and documents, so ransomware cannot hold them hostage.
  • Keep the operating system updated; many attacks exploit flaws that are already patched.

You can try the underlying idea yourself: the Security Lab on this site includes an interactive Grey Wolf Optimizer that shows how a pack of search agents closes in on the best solution.
