Research Statement

The problem

Cybercrime moves faster than signature-based defences

Malware, ransomware and botnets now target phones, IoT devices and cloud hypervisors that traditional tools were never designed to protect. My work asks how machine learning can detect these threats early, explain its decisions, and run on the devices where attacks actually happen.

My approach

Lightweight, explainable and privacy-preserving detection

I combine metaheuristic feature selection (Grey Wolf, Jaya, Harris Hawks, Moth–Flame) with deep and ensemble models to build compact detectors for Android ransomware, IoT botnets and network intrusions. With federated learning I keep data on the device and defend the training process itself against poisoning. Digital forensics grounds the work in evidence that holds up in court.

What's next

LLM-driven threat intelligence and trustworthy edge AI

My current projects fuse multi-source threat intelligence with large language models, detect zero-day malware from system-call sequences with transformers, and bring neuromorphic computing to assistive navigation for visually impaired people. The common aim is AI that is secure, efficient and useful outside the lab.

Teaching philosophy

Learn by doing

Security is a practice. Students attack and defend real systems in labs, analyse live malware samples in sandboxes, and handle evidence the way a court would expect.

Presence and engagement

In blended and online classes I design for cognitive, social and teaching presence: case discussions, peer review and short feedback loops that keep students thinking together.

Research in the classroom

Current research and industry practice feed straight into the curriculum, and strong student projects grow into conference and journal papers.
